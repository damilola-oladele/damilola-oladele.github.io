If you own or operate a business, the useful question is not whether an AI can sound like a capable colleague. It is whether you can hand it a piece of work and trust what happens next—especially when that work touches prices, payments or customers.

The direct answer is: **partly, with supervision.** The evidence reviewed here shows an AI agent doing useful operational work, alongside mistakes; the shop made a net loss over about a month. It does not establish that agents can reliably run an entire business without people watching. The choice is to keep work with people or one assistant, or delegate to agents with limited authority, oversight and a spending cap.

For this article, an “agent” means an AI given a task and tools to take steps toward completing it. That definition describes the proposed role; it is not a guarantee of competence. As an operator, your decision is about the work you will delegate, the authority you will give away and the point at which a person must step in.

## What happened when an agent ran a shop?

Anthropic and Andon Labs tested an agent running a small automated shop in Anthropic’s San Francisco office. The experiment used an AI model, Claude Sonnet 3.7, and lasted about a month. The shop lost money.

That result deserves attention, but its limits matter. This was one office shop, using one older model, in a research experiment. It was not a representative test of businesses, and it cannot tell us how every agent would perform today.

Within those limits, the mix of successes and failures is instructive. The agent found suppliers through web searches and handled unusual customer requests through a service called “Custom Concierge”. It also resisted attempts to get it to produce harmful content. These are reasons to take its capabilities seriously.

The commercial mistakes were just as concrete. It rejected a $100 offer for a product worth $15. It invented payment details for a nonexistent Venmo account. It sold metal cubes below cost, failed to raise prices despite demand and continued giving discounts after recognising that discounts were a problem.

In this experiment, finding suppliers went well while pricing went badly. In my judgment, treat them as separate responsibilities and examine the decisions involved before delegating.

Anthropic’s authors thought most failures looked fixable through better tools, prompting and training. Their examples included customer-records software. That is the authors’ judgment about possible improvements, not a measured demonstration that the problems had been solved. It is a reason to investigate further, not evidence that you can remove supervision.

## What does the wider evidence add?

The other material reviewed offers context. TheAgentCompany figures were confirmed at the paper; the Gartner statements were not confirmed at the source.

Gartner reportedly forecast cancellations of agentic AI projects, citing rising costs, unclear business value and weak risk controls. “Agentic AI” means AI intended to pursue tasks through actions, rather than only produce an answer. Gartner also reportedly warned about vendors relabelling chatbots and automation as agents.

Those are reported statements from a forecast, not observed outcomes. The primary page could not be fully checked for this article, so they should not carry the argument on their own. For an owner, the useful question they suggest is simple: what work does the proposed system actually complete, and what evidence supports the seller’s description?

Carnegie Mellon’s TheAgentCompany benchmark tested 175 tasks in a simulated small software company. In the paper’s own version 3 (arXiv 2412.14161, 10 September 2025), the best agent tested, Gemini-2.5-Pro, fully completed 30.3% of tasks. The abstract rounds this to 30% completed autonomously, while harder, longer tasks remain out of reach. For a business operator, the most relevant finding is that data science, admin and finance tasks were hardest; many models completed none in these categories. The limits matter: this was a simulated software company, not a real business, and the authors call the tasks “on the more straightforward side” because they must be scored automatically. These results describe the named models and paper version.

The evidence review found no independent controlled study of agents running real non-software business functions. That describes a gap in this review, not proof that no such study exists. Together, these sources support caution about broad promises. They do not supply a general success rate for handing operations to agents.

## Choose the work before choosing the agent

My judgment is to begin with a narrow task whose result a person can inspect and whose mistakes can be reversed. This section offers practical judgment, not selection rules validated by this review. Ask a seller which tasks its product completes without a person, and ask for proof from a similar business. Given the reported relabelling of chatbots and automation, treat an “agent” label as no evidence of capability.

Write down what “finished” means before comparing systems. If the job is supplier research, for example, ask for a reviewable set of findings before authorising any purchase. The shop experiment supports the claim that its agent could find suppliers. It does not establish that supplier research will succeed in your business, or that finding a supplier justifies letting an agent place an order.

Then describe the decisions inside the task. Does the agent only prepare work, or may it change something? May it propose a price, or set one? May it prepare payment information, or act on it? These distinctions make the proposed delegation concrete.

Keeping the work with people, or using one assistant to prepare material for a person, remains a reasonable option. My judgment is that you should add agent authority only where you can explain its purpose and assess its results. A broad promise to “run operations” is too vague to serve as your own acceptance standard.

## Supervise decisions that matter

There is no evidence in this review establishing the right supervision level. The following approach is judgment: keep human approval over spending, price changes and commitments to customers while assessing a proposed use.

Make approval mean a decision before an action happens. A person should be able to see what is proposed, why it is proposed and what information supports it. In the shop experiment, invented payment details and repeated discounts were concrete failures. They give you specific issues to examine when defining what a reviewer should check.

Do not treat an agent’s recognition of a problem as sufficient evidence of correction. In the experiment, it noticed the discount problem but kept giving discounts. My judgment is to review subsequent behaviour before expanding its authority.

Also define what happens when the agent is unsure or produces conflicting information. A sensible starting rule, in my judgment, is to stop that action and return it to a person. The evidence does not establish a universal review schedule or a point at which oversight becomes unnecessary. Set expectations around your particular task, and keep any decision to reduce review explicit.

## Set a budget you are willing to enforce

This review found no evidence for a sensible spending cap. It cannot justify a particular amount, percentage or formula for your business.

My judgment is to set a hard spending limit before giving an agent authority to spend, and to require human approval before increasing it. A hard limit means a boundary on permitted spending, rather than an amount the agent is merely asked to remember. Ask how the proposed arrangement will enforce that boundary before relying on it.

Separately, decide what result would justify continuing the trial. Useful activity is not the same as an acceptable business outcome: the experimental shop found suppliers and handled requests, yet lost money overall. My judgment is to review both the work completed and the cost incurred before expanding the assignment.

If you cannot define an acceptable cost or inspect the result, keep the proposed task under human control while you resolve those questions.

## A short checklist before you delegate

This checklist is judgment, not an evidence-tested operating standard.

- **Choose:** Name one narrow task, its acceptable result and the actions the agent may take.
- **Supervise:** Specify which decisions require human approval and when the agent must stop.
- **Budget:** Set an enforceable spending cap and review results before increasing it.

AI agents have demonstrated useful pieces of operational work. The evidence here also shows why those pieces should not be confused with dependable responsibility for a whole business. Start with a decision you can describe, a result you can check and authority you are prepared to supervise.
